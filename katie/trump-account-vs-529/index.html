Trump Account vs. 529

What Emma's Trump Account actually does, why SPYM is being bought automatically, how taxes work, and where a 529 is stronger.

1. Why is everything buying SPYM?

Treasury selected SPYM as the initial default investment. At launch, all Trump Account contributions are invested there automatically.

Treasury has also selected four additional eligible ETFs — IVV, VTI, SPTM and ITOT — and says parents or guardians will be able to allocate across the additional options when that functionality becomes available.

The practical answer: Right now, SPYM-only is an implementation limitation, not necessarily the permanent investment menu.

2. Is this an after-tax account?

Your personal contribution is after-tax. You do not receive a federal income-tax deduction for money you contribute from your own funds.

But this is the crucial distinction:

Trump Account ≠ Roth IRA. The IRS describes a Trump Account as a type of traditional IRA. Family contributions generally create basis, while earnings remain tax-deferred inside the account.

After the growth period, traditional-IRA rules generally apply. That means the account's after-tax basis is not taxed again, but taxable earnings and other pre-tax amounts can be taxed when distributed. Early-distribution rules may also apply unless an exception is available.

3. Can you sell or withdraw?

Two separate questions matter:

  • Withdraw money before the growth period ends: generally no, except for specific statutory exceptions such as certain rollovers, excess-contribution corrections, or death.
  • Change investments inside the account: Treasury has announced additional eligible ETFs and says investment-election functionality is coming.
The account is intentionally designed as a long-term child investment vehicle, not a normal brokerage account.

4. What are the actual benefits?

  • No earned-income requirement during the growth period. Contributions can be made even if the child has no compensation.
  • Tax-deferred compounding. Dividends and gains are not taxed annually while they remain in the account.
  • Very early retirement capital. After the growth period, the account generally transitions into traditional-IRA treatment.
  • Employer contributions can be valuable. A qualifying employer program can contribute up to $2,500 per employee per year, excluded from the employee's gross income, while still counting toward the normal $5,000 annual account limit.
  • Forced long-term investing. The lack of normal early withdrawals can protect the money from being spent prematurely.

The normal annual contribution limit during the growth period is $5,000, subject to inflation adjustments after 2027. Certain government/nonprofit contributions and the federal pilot contribution are excluded from that limit.

6. The biggest tax difference

Imagine $50,000 of contributions eventually grows to $100,000.

529

Used for qualified education

The investment gain can come out federally tax-free when the distribution is used for qualified education expenses.

Trump Account

Traditional-IRA tax structure

After-tax basis is not taxed again, but taxable earnings and other pre-tax amounts are generally subject to traditional-IRA distribution rules.

7. Which bucket is better for what?

529 = education capital

Best aligned with money you realistically expect to spend on qualified education, because the investment growth can be federally tax-free.

Trump Account = long-term capital

More compelling for money intended to compound for decades, especially when employer or other outside contributions are available.

Bottom line: These accounts are better viewed as complementary rather than substitutes. A 529 is usually the more tax-efficient education bucket; a Trump Account is closer to an early-start retirement / long-horizon investment bucket for the child.

8. Two details that are easy to miss

The $1,000 federal pilot contribution

The federal pilot contribution is limited to eligible U.S. citizen children born in 2025, 2026, 2027 or 2028. It does not apply simply because a child has a Trump Account.

529-to-Roth flexibility

Long-standing 529 accounts can potentially make limited tax-free rollovers to the beneficiary's Roth IRA, subject to multiple requirements, including the Roth annual contribution limit, a $35,000 lifetime cap, a 15-year account-age rule, and restrictions on recent contributions.
